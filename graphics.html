Graphics

A guide to the geometry, topology, quantum information and network science illustrated in the site’s background graphics.

Each image is a schematic mathematical construction. Select “View SVG” to inspect a diagram at full size.

  1. Brillouin torus

    Crystal momentum repeats under shifts by reciprocal-lattice vectors. In two dimensions, identifying opposite edges of a fundamental momentum-space cell gives a torus, \(T^2=S^1\times S^1\). The highlighted loops wind around its two independent directions.

    The curved surface is a geometric picture of this periodicity. Band eigenstates vary over the momentum-space torus, and their global structure is central to band topology.

  2. Möbius band

    Joining the ends of a strip after a half twist produces a surface with one boundary component that is non-orientable. Carrying a local normal vector once around its central loop reverses the vector’s direction; a second circuit restores it.

    The band gives a concrete picture of a nontrivial real line bundle: local choices of orientation can be consistent in small patches but fail to extend globally. Related obstructions arise when choosing real eigenvectors in band theory.

  3. Linked Hopf fibres

    In the Hopf fibration \(S^3\to S^2\), the preimage of each point on the two-sphere is a circle. These eight fibres are drawn in ordinary three-dimensional space using stereographic projection; any two distinct fibres link once.

    Linking numbers of preimages give a geometric way to understand Hopf invariants. Related linked structures occur in Hopf and Hopf–Euler band phases.

    Further reading: Non-Abelian Hopf–Euler insulators

  4. Trefoil knot

    This is a trefoil, the simplest nontrivial knot. Its minimum crossing number is three, and it cannot be deformed into an unknotted circle without cutting the strand or passing it through itself.

    Also called the \((2,3)\) torus knot, it can be embedded on a torus. Knot type records a global property of a closed curve that survives smooth deformations, illustrating the distinction between local geometry and global topology.

  5. Bloch sphere

    Ignoring an overall phase, a pure qubit state can be written as

    \[|\psi\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle.\]

    The two angles specify a point on the sphere. The poles are the computational-basis states, and the arrow marks one particular pure state. Mixed qubit states occupy the interior; single-qubit unitary gates act as rotations of the sphere.

    Further reading: IBM Quantum: bits, gates and circuits

  6. Conical band crossing

    The simple two-band Hamiltonian \(H=\hbar v(k_x\sigma_x+k_y\sigma_y)\), where \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_y\) are Pauli matrices, has energies

    \[E_\pm=\pm\hbar v\sqrt{k_x^2+k_y^2}.\]

    The two sheets meet at a Dirac point and disperse linearly nearby. The band gap vanishes at the apex. Such local crossings are basic building blocks for models of topological band structures.

  7. Winding-two texture

    The arrows show \(\mathbf n(\theta)=(\cos 2\theta,\sin 2\theta)\). A circuit around the origin turns the arrow direction twice, giving winding number \(\nu=2\). At the centre the direction has no well-defined limit.

    Winding textures help visualize band nodes in real multiband systems. The Euler class characterizes an entire oriented two-band bundle; this local texture illustrates one ingredient in that topology.

    Further reading: Euler topology in classical spin liquids

  8. Band-node braid

    Each strand follows a node as a parameter is varied. Exchanging nodes creates a braid that remembers the order of the exchanges.

    In real multiband models, nodes in different gaps can carry noncommuting topological charges. Braiding can change which pairs of nodes can annihilate. This schematic illustrates three continuous trajectories and their exchange order, without assigning a particular Hamiltonian or charge to each strand.

    Further reading: Non-Abelian Hopf–Euler insulators

  9. Surface-code stabilizers

    Qubits sit on the lattice edges. A star applies Pauli \(X\) to four qubits meeting at a vertex; a plaquette applies \(Z\) around one face. These checks commute because an X star and a Z plaquette overlap on zero or two qubits.

    Their outcomes reveal error patterns. This drawing shows bulk checks; boundary choices specify a finite planar code.

    Further reading: Topological quantum memory

  10. Toric-code lattice

    The lattice is periodic in both directions, with qubits on its edges. The highlighted loops wind around independent cycles of the torus.

    Products of Pauli operators along noncontractible cycles can act as logical operators while commuting with local checks. This global topology underlies the toric code; the standard code on a torus encodes two logical qubits.

    Further reading: Topological quantum memory

  11. Fano plane

    Seven points and seven lines form the smallest finite projective plane, \(\mathrm{PG}(2,2)\). Each line contains three points, and any two points determine a line. The circle counts as a line in this incidence geometry.

    The labels are the nonzero vectors of \(\mathbb F_2^3\); the three labels on each line sum to zero. These vectors also form the columns of the Hamming-code parity-check matrix, connecting the picture to classical error correction and the seven-qubit Steane code.

    Further reading: IBM Quantum: the Steane code

  12. GHZ preparation circuit

    Starting from \(|0000\rangle\), the Hadamard creates a superposition on the first qubit. The three controlled-NOT gates then generate the entangled state

    \[|\mathrm{GHZ}_4\rangle=\frac{|0000\rangle+|1111\rangle}{\sqrt 2}.\]

    Computational-basis measurements return all zeros or all ones. The coherence between these two components is what distinguishes this quantum state from a classical mixture of the same outcomes.

    Further reading: IBM Quantum: GHZ states

  13. Stabilizer measurement

    Four CNOT gates couple the data to an ancilla initialized in \(|0\rangle\). Measuring the ancilla yields the parity \(Z_1Z_2Z_3Z_4\): even parity gives 0 and odd parity gives 1.

    This extracts a collective stabilizer outcome without measuring the individual data bits, supplying information for an error-correction syndrome.

    Further reading: Topological quantum memory

  14. Kagome lattice

    Corner-sharing triangles form the kagome lattice, with a hexagonal loop highlighted here. For antiferromagnetic Ising spins, each bond favours opposite orientations, but all three bonds of a triangle cannot be satisfied simultaneously.

    This geometric frustration makes kagome systems important models for unusual magnetism and spin-liquid behaviour. The drawing specifies the connectivity; a spin model additionally requires degrees of freedom and interactions.

  15. Band-energy contours

    Each curve joins momenta with the same energy. The illustration uses a two-band model \(H(\mathbf k)=\mathbf d(\mathbf k)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), with \(d_x=\sin k_x\), \(d_y=\sin k_y\) and \(d_z=1.2+\cos k_x+\cos k_y\). Its energies are \(E_\pm=\pm|\mathbf d|\).

    The contours reveal the shape of the dispersion, whose gradients determine group velocities. Band topology also depends on eigenvectors, so the energy contours provide only part of the description.

  16. Temporal network

    The layers are snapshots at successive times. Nodes retain their identities while edges change; dotted lines connect selected copies of the same node across time.

    Comparing snapshots can reveal recurring patterns or changes in system state. This motivates partially temporal network analysis in Phasik, where available time-dependent information is combined with network structure to study how interactions evolve.

    Further reading: Phasik: identifying states in temporal networks
